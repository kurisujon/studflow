# Cellular Respiration

<!-- anchor: cellular-respiration/intro -->
Cellular respiration is a set of metabolic reactions and processes that take place in the cells of organisms to convert biochemical energy from nutrients into adenosine triphosphate (ATP), and then release waste products. The reactions involved in respiration are catabolic reactions, which break large molecules into smaller ones, releasing energy.

<!-- anchor: cellular-respiration/stages -->
The major stages of cellular respiration are glycolysis, the citric acid cycle (also known as the Krebs cycle), and oxidative phosphorylation. These stages progressively extract energy from glucose and other organic fuels.

<!-- anchor: cellular-respiration/glycolysis -->
Glycolysis takes place in the cytoplasm. In this phase, a six-carbon glucose molecule is split into two three-carbon molecules of pyruvate. This process does not require oxygen and yields a net of 2 ATP and 2 NADH molecules per glucose molecule.

<!-- anchor: cellular-respiration/krebs -->
The citric acid cycle occurs in the mitochondrial matrix. The pyruvate from glycolysis is converted into Acetyl-CoA, which then enters the cycle. The citric acid cycle completes the breakdown of glucose by oxidizing a derivative of pyruvate to carbon dioxide.

<!-- anchor: cellular-respiration/oxidative-phosphorylation -->
Oxidative phosphorylation accounts for most of the ATP synthesis. It occurs in the inner mitochondrial membrane and uses the electron transport chain and chemiosmosis. Oxygen acts as the final electron acceptor in the electron transport chain, which is why this phase is strictly aerobic. This stage generates up to 34 ATP molecules.

<!-- anchor: cellular-respiration/anaerobic -->
In the absence of oxygen, certain cells can continue to produce ATP through anaerobic respiration or fermentation. Lactic acid fermentation is common in human muscle cells during strenuous exercise when oxygen delivery is insufficient.
